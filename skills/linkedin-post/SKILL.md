# LinkedIn feed post

This skill is built on the Meltwater x LinkedIn study "How LinkedIn Content Wins in AI Search" (May 2026, 9.5M AI citations across ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot and Google AI). LinkedIn is the #2 source AI models cite, after YouTube. A good post today is written for people and for AI answers.

## 1. Decide who posts
- Individual profiles get 75% of LinkedIn's AI citations and company pages get 25%. Founders (7.5%) and CEOs (8.2%) are the most-cited roles, but expertise beats title.
- Default to the expert posting under their own name, with the company page backing them up. For example, the page writes its own post and the expert comments, or the other way round.
- Use the company page for announcements, events and spotlights. Even then, name and tag the people involved.
- Check that the author's headline and title are specific, because AI models read profile signals.

## 2. Gather facts first
- Pull real details from sources: event pages, emails, notes, photos and profiles. Never invent numbers, quotes, titles or names.
- Specifics get cited: names, dates, places, prices, numbers and tools. If a person wants to stay anonymous or vague, respect that over specificity.
- Prefer first-hand detail: what happened, what was said, what was learned.

## 3. Write it
- **Hook:** the first 1–2 lines carry the post and must make sense on their own before "…more". Lead with the point, the news or a sharp question.
- **Answer a real question:** frame the post around something your audience would ask an AI, and answer it directly.
- **Structure:** short paragraphs. Use a numbered or bulleted list whenever there are 3+ parallel items. Every top-cited piece in the study used lists.
- **Original, not reshared:** 72% of cited content is original. Instead of a bare repost, write your own post that links to the original and adds your perspective. This also helps when a post can't be reposted, for example when it's visible to connections only.
- **Length:** about 80–250 words for most posts. Anything longer belongs in an article (use the LinkedIn article skill).
- **Tone:** plain, warm and specific. Avoid buzzword chains and corporate filler.
- **Close:** one clear call to action (RSVP link, question to reply to, link to the full article). Put links at the end.
- **Hashtags:** 0–3 relevant ones, at the end.

## 4. Tag everyone
- Every person and organization named gets a real @-mention, picked from LinkedIn's mention dropdown, not typed as plain text.
- If an entity has no LinkedIn page, tag its closest real partner organization instead, and note that in the draft.
- Check that names are spelled exactly as on their profiles.

## 5. Review and approve
- Show the user the full draft, with the tag list and the account it will post as (person or page), and wait for explicit approval. Never post without it.
- When posting through the browser, choose the correct actor (personal profile or page admin), add the mentions from the dropdown, post once, then confirm the live URL and take a screenshot.
- For a delayed follow-up (such as a reply comment X minutes later), create a one-off routine and confirm it saved. Don't promise a timed post you haven't scheduled.

## 6. Keep it fresh
- 48% of cited content is under 3 months old, and only 12% is over a year old. Post consistently: aim for 2–3 posts a week from active experts.
- Turn strong posts into articles, and update evergreen posts with new numbers instead of letting them go stale.

## Checklist
- [ ] The hook works on its own
- [ ] It answers a real question with specifics
- [ ] It has a list where there are 3+ items
- [ ] Every person and organization is @-tagged
- [ ] No invented facts
- [ ] It's original rather than a bare repost
- [ ] It has one call to action, with links at the end
- [ ] The posting account is correct
- [ ] The user approved the exact text
