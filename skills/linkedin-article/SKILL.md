# LinkedIn article

This skill is built on the Meltwater x LinkedIn study "How LinkedIn Content Wins in AI Search" (May 2026, 9.5M AI citations). Articles and plain text posts make up 83% of the LinkedIn content AI models cite. Articles are the format most likely to become the answer an AI gives. For short updates, use the LinkedIn post skill instead.

## 1. Pick a topic AI gets asked about
- Start from a real question a buyer or peer would type into ChatGPT or Google, and make the title answer it.
- The formats that win most:
  - Ranked lists of tools or vendors (54% of the most-cited content), such as "7 AI tools we actually use for X, ranked"
  - Decision frameworks, such as "How to choose…"
  - Comparisons, such as "X vs. Y for Z"
  - Step-by-step guides
  - Lessons learned from real work
- LinkedIn is strongest in B2B topics: technology and SaaS, consulting and professional services, finance, marketing, and HR and talent.
- The author should be the person with the expertise, writing under their own name. Individuals get 75% of citations. Publish from a company page only for institutional pieces.

## 2. Research
- Collect real sources, data, examples and quotes. Cite or link every number. Never invent statistics, quotes or results.
- Specific names, prices, numbers and dates are what get quoted. Generic thought leadership isn't.
- Add first-hand experience that no one else has. 72% of cited content is original.

## 3. Structure
- **Title:** a clear, answer-shaped title that includes the key phrase people search for.
- **Opening (2–4 sentences):** state the answer or thesis up front, then say who it's for.
- **Headings:** use H2 headings for each section. 92% of top-cited posts had clear headings.
- **Lists:** every top-cited article used bulleted or numbered lists. Use them for steps, criteria, rankings and takeaways.
- **Optional elements:** a short summary box near the top, or a comparison table.
- **Length:** about 800–1,500 words. Keep paragraphs short and scannable.
- **Close:** a summary of key takeaways, then one call to action (contact, event, newsletter, discussion question).

## 4. Tag and credit
- @-mention every person and organization named, picked from LinkedIn's mention dropdown. If an organization has no page, tag the closest real partner organization.
- Credit sources and collaborators by name.

## 5. Package it
- Use a cover image that's relevant and not stock-generic, with the right dimensions for LinkedIn articles.
- Draft a companion feed post (using the LinkedIn post skill) that gives the hook and 3 key points and links to the article.
- Plan for experts and members to comment or reshare with their own commentary, not a bare repost.

## 6. Review and approve
- Give the user the full draft (as a doc if it's long), plus the title, the author account, the tag list and the companion post. Wait for explicit approval before publishing.
- After publishing, confirm the live URL and take a screenshot.

## 7. Keep it fresh
- 48% of cited content is under 3 months old, and only 12% is older than a year. Revisit evergreen articles every quarter, update numbers and examples, and note "Updated <month year>".
- Cadence target for an active expert: 1–4 articles a month (the study suggests 3–4) alongside 2–3 feed posts a week. Scale this down for small teams.

## Checklist
- [ ] The title answers a real question
- [ ] The answer comes first
- [ ] Every section has an H2 heading
- [ ] It has lists
- [ ] Specifics are cited
- [ ] There are no invented facts
- [ ] It includes original first-hand insight
- [ ] Everyone is @-tagged
- [ ] It has a cover image and a companion post
- [ ] The user approved it
